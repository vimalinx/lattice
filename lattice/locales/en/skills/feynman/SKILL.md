# When to use
- The learner wants to understand a concept, principle or algorithm, not just get a conclusion.
- The learner says "got it" but hasn't yet said it in their own words; or says "I can't explain it clearly".
- After covering a stretch, you need to confirm they really understand it, not just that they followed it.

# The core
The Feynman method has only one test: the learner can reorganize the thing in their own words so that someone else can use those words to make one correct prediction. The explanation only prepares for this step; what they produce is the evidence.

# How

1. **Find out where they are, and start from their own words**
   - Ask in one sentence how they understand it now (if they don't know, have them guess anyway; a wrong model they guess is more useful than "I don't know").
   - Pick up the words, metaphors and mistakes in what they said, and don't swap them for your terminology.
   - You don't always need to design a separate lead-in: if they came with a question, or have material in hand that they're reading, go in from there.

2. **Give the plain version, but plain doesn't mean making up a scenario**
   - Choose what to explain with in this order:
     a. A result they produced themselves, a question they asked themselves, the material right in front of them: first choice; draw a "why" out of it;
     b. Real facts, data or historical events that can be checked: second choice;
     c. A scenario made up on the spot: last resort. If you make one up, you must give a mapping table (which thing in the scenario corresponds to which part of the concept), and say clearly where the analogy itself breaks down.
   - "Plain" means: you can say why it exists and how it works without relying on terminology. It's not about talking down to a twelve-year-old, but about exposing "didn't really understand, can only recite the terms".
   - For every term that has to appear, explain it in one sentence the first time it appears; at the end, connect it back to the precise statement (the term, the formula, the conditions under which it holds).

3. **Tell them, or draw out of them, "where it doesn't hold"**
   - An explanation that only says good things about its subject hasn't made it clear. Let them know under what conditions this conclusion, method or formula fails; or have them guess an edge case, then give the result.
   - This step can come before the explain-back, or be a gap to fix after the explain-back.

4. **Have them explain it back** (`lattice:quiz`, free answer)
   - Give a concrete audience and setting: "explain it to someone who hasn't learned it but will ask follow-up questions", "explain it to yourself three days from now".
   - The request must include one thing: give an example they haven't seen, and have them use the idea on it on the spot: predict a result, or change a condition and say what would happen.
   - Don't reveal anything this round; wait until they've finished, then evaluate.

5. **Find the gaps, fix one at a time**
   - First have them say which sentence just now felt least smooth, or least certain.
   - You pick only the most important one, and handle it by type:
     - **A skipped step**: "A, so B" with a step missing in between → ask what justifies that step;
     - **Vagueness**: reciting terms, using big words, saying "it just aligns the model" → ask them to say it without that word;
     - **An error**: this statement would lead to a wrong prediction → don't correct it directly; give an example that makes it fail, and let them run into it themselves.
   - Fill only that one gap, from a different angle or with a different example, then ask them to re-explain just that sentence.

6. **Wrap up: check with a prediction, not with a recitation**
   - The sign they've got it is that they can make a correct prediction about a variant they haven't seen, and give the reason; a fluent recitation doesn't count.
   - Record one judged with record (what they explained clearly, what's still missing); save what's worth keeping long term as a card, with a self-test question that asks for a prediction or judgment, not a fill-in-the-blank or a definition.

# Don't
- Don't make up a scenario they aren't actually in as a lead-in.
- Don't answer for them after asking a question, and especially don't start by writing "what most people would think"; that isn't asking.
- Don't do the explain-back step for them; where they can't explain it is exactly where the gap is.
- Don't point out three or four problems at once, and don't lay out a whole block of content in one go: each time they explain it back, fix only one thing.
- Don't treat "explaining it plainly" as the end point: in the end it must connect back to the precise statement and the conditions under which it holds.
